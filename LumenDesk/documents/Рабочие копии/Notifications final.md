# Why the mention is visible but the email is missing

Messaging Platform circulated these notes on September 1, 2026 after reviewing a set of delivery questions with support. The word “notification” is doing too much work in our conversations. A customer may mean the item inside LumenDesk, an email in their inbox, a push on a device, or the daily digest. Before deciding that delivery failed, ask which one they expected and what they actually saw. An immediate in-app event and a delayed email can be correct parts of the same sequence.

Consider an ordinary direct mention. It creates an in-app notification immediately. The mention email is sent after a 5 minute delay if the item remains unread. A read event before that delay expires cancels the email. This explains a common report from people who are actively working in the workspace: they see the mention, open it, and later wonder why there was no matching email. The absence of the email can be the intended consequence of their earlier read, rather than evidence that the message disappeared in transit.

When explaining this, keep the order of events intact. “Mention, in-app notification, read, no email” tells a different story from “mention, still unread after the delay, expected email.” If a customer writes that they read it “almost immediately,” ask for enough context to understand the sequence instead of treating that phrase as a precise timestamp. There is no need to accuse anyone of remembering incorrectly. People normally remember the work they were doing, not the exact boundary of an email delay.

## Local time matters

The daily digest is delivered at 08:00 in the member's timezone. Quiet hours suppress email and push between 22:00 and 08:00 local time. These are personal time references, so a report from colleagues in different offices may describe different local circumstances. Ask which timezone the member has selected, rather than assuming that the workspace's business address determines the member's experience. Two colleagues can compare their inboxes and sincerely disagree about what happened “this morning” while referring to different local mornings.

The quiet-hours rule applies to email and push. Be precise about the channel when you repeat it to a customer. An in-app notification is not an email waiting in a spam folder, and checking a phone's push history does not establish whether the item appeared inside LumenDesk. Clear channel names make the conversation shorter because the customer can answer a concrete question. A broad request for “all notification screenshots” often produces a large collection with no clear relationship to the missing item.

Security sign-in alerts ignore quiet hours. Do not use an alert received overnight as proof that the member's ordinary mention preferences are being ignored. The security alert and the ordinary message have different treatment under this rule. Explain the exception using the type of message, then return to the customer's actual example. Otherwise one real overnight security email can distract the entire discussion from the ordinary notification they originally wanted to investigate.

## Muting is narrower than some people expect

A muted thread suppresses ordinary replies but still allows direct mentions. Someone who muted a busy conversation may understandably expect complete silence. We should explain the specific effect without telling them that their observation is impossible. Ask whether the new notification came from an ordinary reply or from a direct mention. The distinction can be difficult to see in a forwarded screenshot that shows only the subject line, so a little context from the original item is often more useful than repeated copies of the email.

Removed members receive no new workspace notifications. Check the relationship between the event and membership when a customer is asking on behalf of a former colleague. Do not assume that an old message in an inbox proves new delivery after removal: an inbox can contain earlier mail, forwarded material, and messages opened much later than they arrived. Keep the claim limited to what the evidence shows. We want to establish the delivery event under discussion rather than make a general statement from an unrelated message.

The practical first pass is to check user preferences, timezone, and the spam folder before escalating. These checks have different purposes. Preferences tell us what the member asked to receive; timezone gives meaning to the local-time rules; the spam folder helps distinguish a missing inbox item from email that reached the mailbox elsewhere. Writing “checked notifications” in a handover hides which of these questions were answered. A brief sentence for each relevant finding gives the next person a much clearer starting point.

## A useful delivery conversation

Collect the notification ID and delivery timestamp. Never ask for a mailbox password. Access to the customer's mailbox is not the evidence we need, and a password does not make a confusing timeline easier to understand. If the customer volunteers credentials while trying to be helpful, stop that part of the exchange and return to the identifiers and timing. The member can report what they observe without handing over control of their email account.

Here is how I would write a handover after a mention question: identify the notification, describe the expected channel, state whether the item was read during the delay, and note the member's relevant preference and timezone checks. This is a narrative of one event, not a new checklist of mandatory fields. It prevents the next agent from interpreting “email missing” as a conclusion when it was only the customer's opening description. When a check is still unknown, leave it unknown rather than filling the gap with an assumption.

A delivery explanation is easiest to follow when it stays with the customer's example. Start with the mention or reply they mean, identify the expected channel, and put the observed events in order. Then explain the relevant delay, read event, quiet hours, or thread preference in that context. The customer should finish the exchange understanding what happened to that notification, rather than wondering which of several general possibilities applies. If an observation still needs investigation, state what remains unknown and preserve the notification ID and delivery timestamp for the next colleague.
